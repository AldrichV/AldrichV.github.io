## The question

Hydroelectric dams and wind turbines are well established, but neither has proven able to power a city alone. Solar radiation is among the most abundant and consistent energy sources we have. The report asked: **can solar panels generate electricity at the scale cities need, and can they cut the power industry's impact on the environment?** In short, can solar replace or improve today's infrastructure?

## What was done

I assessed current solar panel technology on three fronts, using published case studies for each:

1. **Environmental impact:** a panel's life cycle, from its components to the end of its life.
2. **Economic impact:** whether solar can generate enough to profit, its "payback time", and the cost of production.
3. **Availability:** how much sunlight different regions get, and who actually has access to the technology.

I then looked at energy storage as a way past solar's biggest limitation.

## The data

**How far the technology has come.** Bell Laboratories introduced the first public solar panel in 1954. It was 4% efficient and could only power small toys. Today's panels reach almost **30%** and can power buildings and vehicles.

**What a panel is made of.** Its parts are:

- **Photovoltaic cells:** made of silicon, which makes up about 28% of the Earth's crust. The cells wear out with prolonged exposure to sunlight, and coolants extend their life.
- **Glass or a clear polymer top layer:** mass-produced.
- **EVA polymer:** the glue that holds the cells in place. It is mass-produced too, but breaks down quickly in extreme weather.
- **Copper or silver contacts:** these carry the current and can be recovered from old electronics.

**What the case studies showed:**

| Case study | Result |
|---|---|
| Rooftop PV for one faculty at Tashkent State Technical University | Saved about €50 a month, or about €15,000 over the panels' 25-year life |
| Recycling materials from scrapped panels and devices | Raised the profit of making and selling panels by about **35.2%** |
| Resource taxes in Spain | Fell steeply as demand for silicon, copper and polymers grew |
| Payback time across cities | Shorter in sunnier cities, longer in smoggy ones, so many large, polluted cities wait longer |
| Average daily solar insolation (NSRDB) | About 4.5 kWh/m² in North America and 5.5 kWh/m² in South America |
| Battery storage added to a solar farm in San Francisco, over one year | Operating costs down **29.3%** |

## What it showed

**Clean to make.** A panel's components are either abundant or recyclable, so producing panels has little environmental impact. Developing polymers that decompose would make that even better.

**Cheap to make, profitable to run**, as long as the location is right. Payback depends on weather, smog and the cost of installation. Larger communities couldn't sustain themselves on solar without some form of storage. The San Francisco study showed storage pays for itself, and an algorithm trained on more data could tune it further.

**Availability is the real limit.** Output depends on clear skies and daylight hours. In weaker climates, panels can be adapted: by catching sunlight reflected off snow, or with cells more sensitive to ultraviolet light so they work better in haze. Many developing countries have ideal sunlight but run on diesel because they lack the infrastructure. With access to the technology, the research suggests they could become self-sufficient and cut a large share of the world's pollution.

**Conclusion:** solar can carry much more of the load if solar farms are paired with storage and placed by cost-benefit analysis where the insolation is highest, including in the countries that could benefit most.
