## The question

The 737 MAX 7 flies short-haul routes, which means a lot of takeoffs and landings. Each one loads the landing gear: it carries the aircraft's weight on the ground and absorbs the impact of touchdown. This report asked **how that gear is manufactured now, and how its design and manufacture are likely to change by 2045.**

## What was done

A short research report in three parts:

- **The system:** what the gear assembly is made of and what each part does.
- **Today's process:** the materials and manufacturing steps, and why each one is used.
- **The future:** where landing gear design is heading, which manufacturing methods would make it possible, and what that would save.

## The data

**The assembly.** A shock strut uses compressed gas and hydraulic oil to absorb landing energy. The main fitting, drag strut and side stay carry loads into the airframe, and a torque link keeps the wheel aligned. A retract jack and pivot linkages fold the gear into the fuselage to cut drag. The wheels use multi-disc carbon brakes.

**How it's made today:**

| Step | What it does |
|---|---|
| Material choice | Titanium alloys, high-strength steels and aluminum alloys, chosen for repeated high loads |
| Closed-die forging | A heated billet is pressed into a shaped die. The grain structure gets refined and lines up with the part's shape, which raises strength and fatigue resistance. |
| Precision CNC machining | Brings the forging to tight tolerances so it fits the assembly |
| Heat treatment | Quenching and tempering add strength, hardness and fatigue life |
| Surface treatment | Shot peening and protective coatings resist fatigue cracking and corrosion |

**What's coming by 2045:**

- **Lighter materials:** advanced titanium alloys and carbon fiber composites, to save weight and fuel.
- **Embedded sensors:** monitoring stress, vibration and temperature to enable predictive maintenance.
- **Electric taxi:** motors in the wheels, cutting fuel use, emissions and noise on the ground.

## What it showed

The most promising manufacturing change is **lightweight lattice structures**. Additive manufacturing, combined with metal forming and extrusion, can build internal geometries that forging and machining can't. Topology optimization decides where material goes, based on the real load paths, so it only sits where it's needed. The part comes out lighter but just as strong.

The payoff is mostly economic. Lighter gear lowers fuel burn, operating cost and emissions. Sensor-driven maintenance cuts downtime. Automated, additive production makes the parts more efficiently. For an aircraft that lands as often as the MAX 7, those savings add up over its whole life.
