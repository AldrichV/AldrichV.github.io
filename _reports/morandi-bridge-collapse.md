## The question

In 2018, a 200 m stretch of the Morandi Bridge in Genoa collapsed, along with pylon 9, one of its three supporting towers. 43 people were killed and 16 injured. The case study asked three things:

- **The cause:** what made the bridge fail.
- **The ethics:** what the people responsible should have done differently.
- **The law:** what legal consequences should follow, and who would pay.

## What was done

We worked in three stages:

- **Background research:** news reports, engineering write-ups and the trial coverage, used to build up the timeline, the cause and the parties involved.
- **EGAD ethics analysis:** taking Autostrade per l'Italia (the operator) as the protagonist, we named its ethical failures and tied each one to the professional-misconduct (s. 72) and code-of-ethics (s. 77) clauses of the regulation under Ontario's Professional Engineers Act. We then generated alternative actions, checked each against the same clauses, and chose one.
- **Legal analysis:** we identified the legal concept, tested it against the facts, and predicted the ruling. My part was the last step: how the court would likely decide, who would be ordered to pay, and for what.

## The data

**Why it fell.** The first expert reports found corrosion in the steel strands of the stay cables on pylon 9. It was a long-term problem, driven by moisture together with salty sea air and pollution from the factories below. Several design choices made it worse:

- **Few cables, all hidden:** each tower had only four stay cables, all encased in prestressed concrete. Modern cable-stayed bridges use dozens of exposed cables, which can be inspected. On the Morandi Bridge the corrosion couldn't be seen.
- **Faster aging than planned:** Morandi himself flagged this in a 1979 report. He also noted that traffic was well above what the bridge was designed for.
- **Constant repair:** the roadway had been under repair since the 1970s, when it began to bend and twist. Only one major repair to a stay was ever made, in the 1990s, and experts had been calling for a full replacement.
- **The weather that day:** high winds and a heavy storm.

**Who was involved.** The parties were:

- Autostrade per l'Italia, the operator.
- Spea Engineering, its inspection and maintenance subsidiary.
- Outside engineering consultants.
- Italy's Ministry of Infrastructure and Transport.
- The victims' families, as plaintiffs.

In April 2023, a judge accepted a €30 million plea bargain from Autostrade and Spea. Prosecutors argued the defendants knew the bridge was at risk and skipped maintenance to save money. The former Autostrade CEO's defence was that it was an original construction defect.

## What it showed

**The cause was corrosion nobody could see.** The design hid the stay cables inside concrete, and that hid the damage that eventually broke the southern tie rod on the Genoa side of pylon 9.

**The ethical call.** We judged the operator's failures as negligence, failure to safeguard life, failure to report a danger to the public, and failure to follow regulations. Of the five alternatives we generated, the best was to **maintain and inspect the bridge far more often**. Proper inspection would have shown that the supports needed replacing with corrosion-resistant material, and the bridge could have been closed for repair. That option broke the fewest clauses, because it goes straight at the real problem: negligence.

**The legal outcome.** This is a tort (negligence) case. The people of Genoa were owed a duty of care. Autostrade breached it by leaving known defects unfixed and not warning the public, and the collapse caused deaths, injuries and losses. The Florida International University footbridge collapse, also in 2018, served as a precedent. We concluded a court would hold **Autostrade per l'Italia** mainly responsible and order it to pay damages to:

- **The victims' families:** for the 43 people killed.
- **The injured:** for hospital bills and lost work.
- **Personal property:** damaged buildings and the many vehicles lost.
- **Reconstruction:** demolition of the old bridge and building the new one.
- **Local businesses:** losses from the disruption.
- **Wider trade:** the disruption to national and international routes that ran over the bridge.

Architect Renzo Piano was asked to design the replacement four months after the collapse. It has been open since 2020 and was designed around parts that are easy to service.
