## The idea

Community centres and schools are full of motion that goes nowhere: people on exercise bikes, elevators running all day, doors opening and closing. Most people are also a long way from where their electricity comes from. Working with a community partner, our team looked at capturing some of that motion and, just as importantly, making the generation visible.

## Why small and local

Centralised grids are vulnerable to extreme weather and equipment failure, and the usual backup is a fossil-fuel generator. Small hand-powered generators exist, but they're built for emergencies, not for daily use in a public space. We wanted something people would use because it's there and it's interesting.

## What we built

A hand-crank generator in three parts:

- **The crank:** a 3D-printed wheel and housing turning a generator motor. Printing let us iterate the housing quickly and keep rotation smooth.
- **Regulation:** a rectifier with diodes and capacitors turns the output into usable, storable DC.
- **Feedback:** indicators so users can see what they're producing.

In testing it averaged about **7–8 V**, enough to show that small-scale recovery works.

## Would it scale?

Feasibility calculations put high-use systems like elevators and gym equipment at roughly **0.4–0.45 kWh a day** each. That won't run a building, but it offsets real consumption and makes the point visible. The design is modular, so piezoelectric, solar or small wind inputs could be added later.

## Telling the story

Half the brief was education. We made a poster and an animated video for the community partner so visitors could understand what the generator does and why it matters.
