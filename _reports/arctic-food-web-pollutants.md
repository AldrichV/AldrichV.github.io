## The question

The Arctic is where climate change and pollution hit hardest. Top predators such as polar bears, beluga whales and seals eat a lot to survive, so whatever their prey has absorbed builds up in them. That makes them good indicators of an ecosystem's health. This review asked **how persistent pollutants reach Arctic food webs, and what they do to the animals at the top.**

## What was done

We reviewed three bodies of field research, one for each class of pollutant, and compared what each found about how the contaminant moves and what harm it does:

- **Mercury:** a toxic metal from industrial emissions.
- **PCBs:** polychlorinated biphenyls, a classic persistent organic pollutant (POP).
- **Current-use pesticides (CUPs):** the newer pesticides that replaced DDT after it was restricted in the 1970s.

## The data

| Study | What was sampled | What was found |
|---|---|---|
| Mercury and POPs in polar bears (Svalbard, Norway) | Blood from 23 male polar bears, aged 3 to 21 | Higher testosterone went with better body condition, but POPs were interfering with the hormone pathways that matter for reproduction. The imbalance depended on age, and adult males showed higher ratios than young males. |
| PCBs in glaucous gulls (Bjørnøya, July 1996) | 40 adult gulls, examined for intestinal parasites | 1–32 ppm of PCBs in liver tissue, about **three orders of magnitude** higher than in the fish and crustaceans they eat. PCBs suppressed their immune systems, which led to more infections. |
| Pesticides across the Canadian Arctic Archipelago | Plants, invertebrates, fish, seals and polar bears | The highest concentrations were in plants, invertebrates, fish and seals, and the lowest in polar bears. Polar bears break down endosulfan into endosulfan sulphate and clear it from their bodies, but seals build it up to dangerous levels. |

The pesticide study pointed to an evolutionary weak spot. Marine mammals such as whales, dolphins and seals have lost the enzyme that breaks down these chemicals. They can't clear the pesticides out, so the chemicals disrupt their endocrine systems and cause developmental and reproductive problems.

## What it showed

The three pollutants take the same route. They are released far away, carried north by air and ocean currents, then concentrated step by step from plankton and small fish up to seals, gulls and bears. The harm varies by pollutant, from reproductive and hormone disruption to immune suppression and changes in behaviour.

The larger finding was about **food-web stability**. Contaminants don't only harm individual animals. The review suggests pesticides may be killing seals before they're ever eaten. That would leave polar bears short of food and slowly shrinking in number, even though the bears themselves handle the chemicals well. Weakening the top predators threatens the balance of the whole ecosystem. This is the case for stronger global limits on pollutant emissions, and for continued monitoring of what reaches the Arctic.
